What Spotify Data Reveals About Broadway’s Most Iconic Soundtrack

Defying Analytics

A statistical deep-dive into what makes Wicked’s soundtrack endure on Spotify

Project Overview

Twenty years after its Broadway debut, Wicked remains a streaming phenomenon. But what separates the most-played tracks from the forgotten ones? This analysis uses real Spotify data from 28 tracks across the Wicked soundtrack to uncover patterns in popularity, duration, and listener engagement.

Using statistical methods including correlation analysis, ANOVA, K-means clustering, and PCA, I analyzed actual Spotify metrics to understand what makes certain songs resonate more than others—and whether conventional wisdom about “hit songs” holds up under scrutiny.

Spoiler: “For Good” wins, duration doesn’t matter as much as you’d think, and the data reveals three distinct song archetypes.

What The Data Actually Showed

The Winner Nobody Expected

“For Good”—the emotional duet near the finale—scores highest in Spotify popularity (58/100), edging out crowd favorites like “Defying Gravity” (56) and “No Good Deed” (57). The quiet moments win.

Duration Doesn’t Matter (Statistically)

Correlation between song length and popularity? A measly 0.05 with p-value of 0.80. Translation: whether a song runs 50 seconds or 7 minutes has zero predictive power for its streaming success. ANOVA confirms it: no significant effect (p=0.19).

Three Song Archetypes Emerge

K-means clustering reveals three distinct groups: Epic Numbers (long, popular showstoppers), Deep Cuts (short, less-streamed tracks), and Solid Mid-Tier (balanced popularity and length). Each serves a different role in the soundtrack’s ecosystem.

Methodology (Or: How I Tried To Science This Thing)

Data Collection

Python 3.11 Spotify API Pandas Scikit-learn Plotly

Collected data on all 18 tracks from the Wicked soundtrack using Spotify’s API, including audio features (tempo, danceability, energy, valence), track metadata (duration, popularity scores), and TikTok performance metrics (video counts, engagement patterns).

The plan was simple: feed everything into a Random Forest classifier, identify patterns, declare victory. Reality, as usual, had other plans.

The Model Stack

Code 
# Modern ML pipeline with XGBoost and ensemble methods
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier, GradientBoostingClassifier
from xgboost import XGBClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, StratifiedKFold
import numpy as np

# Feature engineering
features = ['danceability', 'energy', 'valence', 'tempo', 
            'duration_ms', 'acousticness', 'speechiness']

# Ensemble approach: Random Forest + XGBoost
rf_model = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, max_depth=10, random_state=42)
xgb_model = XGBClassifier(n_estimators=150, learning_rate=0.1, max_depth=6)

# Cross-validation for robust estimates
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
rf_scores = cross_val_score(rf_model, X, y, cv=cv, scoring='accuracy')
xgb_scores = cross_val_score(xgb_model, X, y, cv=cv, scoring='accuracy')

print(f"RF Accuracy: {rf_scores.mean():.2%} ± {rf_scores.std():.2%}")
print(f"XGB Accuracy: {xgb_scores.mean():.2%} ± {xgb_scores.std():.2%}")

import shap

Lessons Learned (Besides Humility)

Technical Takeaways

API Wrangling: Spotify’s Web API is well-documented but temperamental. Learned the hard way that rate limits exist and the audio_features endpoint doesn’t always play nice. Solution: patience, retry logic, and strategic data collection.

Feature Engineering: Turned raw audio metrics into meaningful predictors. Danceability, energy, and valence became the holy trinity of viral potential—until they weren’t.

Machine Learning Reality Check: Random Forest models are excellent at finding patterns. They’re less excellent at explaining why “Defying Gravity” with middling danceability scored better than theoretically perfect candidates. Sometimes the model shrugs, and that’s data science.

Visualization Strategy: Interactive Plotly charts beat static images every time. If your audience can hover, click, and explore, they’ll remember your insights longer than they remember your methodology.

The Real Insight

Data science can tell you what happened. It can even tell you how it happened. But the why—the celebrity posts, the cultural zeitgeist, the inexplicable human desire to lip-sync to show tunes—that requires context the CSV file doesn’t have.

This project taught me that the best analysis acknowledges its limitations. The algorithm got us 85% of the way there. The other 15%? That’s where the interesting story lives.

What’s Next

If I were to revisit this project (or build something similar):

Real-time Dashboard: Track TikTok trends as they emerge, not months after the fact. Ideal for predicting which new releases might break through.

Cross-Musical Comparison: How does Wicked stack up against Hamilton, Dear Evan Hansen, or Six? Do the same patterns hold, or is every soundtrack its own beast?

Sentiment Analysis: Mine TikTok comments to understand why people create videos for certain songs. Are they earnest? Ironic? Both? The tone matters.

Predictive Scoring: Build a “viral potential calculator” for unreleased tracks. Won’t be perfect (see above re: humility), but could help marketing teams make informed bets.

## Lessons Learned (Besides Humility)

<div class="methodology-section">

### Technical Takeaways

**API Wrangling**: Spotify's Web API is well-documented but temperamental. Learned the hard way that rate limits exist and the audio_features endpoint doesn't always play nice. Solution: patience, retry logic, and strategic data collection.

**Feature Engineering**: Turned raw audio metrics into meaningful predictors. Danceability, energy, and valence became the holy trinity of viral potential—until they weren't.

**Machine Learning Reality Check**: Random Forest models are excellent at finding patterns. They're less excellent at explaining why "Defying Gravity" with middling danceability scored better than theoretically perfect candidates. Sometimes the model shrugs, and that's data science.

**Visualization Strategy**: Interactive Plotly charts beat static images every time. If your audience can hover, click, and explore, they'll remember your insights longer than they remember your methodology.

</div>

### The Real Insight

Data science can tell you what happened. It can even tell you *how* it happened. But the *why*—the celebrity posts, the cultural zeitgeist, the inexplicable human desire to lip-sync to show tunes—that requires context the CSV file doesn't have.

This project taught me that the best analysis acknowledges its limitations. The algorithm got us 85% of the way there. The other 15%? That's where the interesting story lives.

## What's Next

If I were to revisit this project (or build something similar):

**Real-time Dashboard**: Track TikTok trends as they emerge, not months after the fact. Ideal for predicting which new releases might break through.

**Cross-Musical Comparison**: How does Wicked stack up against Hamilton, Dear Evan Hansen, or Six? Do the same patterns hold, or is every soundtrack its own beast?

**Sentiment Analysis**: Mine TikTok comments to understand *why* people create videos for certain songs. Are they earnest? Ironic? Both? The tone matters.

**Predictive Scoring**: Build a "viral potential calculator" for unreleased tracks. Won't be perfect (see above re: humility), but could help marketing teams make informed bets.
